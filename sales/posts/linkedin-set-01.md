## B2 · From 14 minutes down to six (proof)

```
14 minutes. That's how long a new user needed to reach a first trade on a trading platform I redesigned.

After the redesign: about 6.

First-session trade completion went from 52% to 71%.

What changed was not the visual style. It was the order of the steps.

The old flow asked for everything up front: identity, funding, risk profile, preferences. Only then could you trade. By the time users reached the market, half of them had left.

The new flow asked for the minimum to show a real price, let the user pick an asset, and moved verification to the moment it was needed. Same regulation, same data, different sequence.

Three questions I ask about every onboarding now:
1. What is the first real win, and how many screens away is it?
2. Which steps are required by law, and which are just habit?
3. Where does the user wait without knowing why?

How long does your product take to deliver its first win?

Save this for your next onboarding review.
```

## B3 · Open the first win (opinion)

```
In fintech, every minute before the first win is a minute for doubt.

The user has handed over a passport scan, a phone number, maybe a bank login. They've received nothing yet. Trust is borrowed, and the clock is running.

Most teams treat that gap as a compliance problem. It's a design problem.

A few things I've seen work:
- Show the product before asking for the identity check. A real price, a real portfolio view, a real balance preview.
- Explain each request in one sentence. "We need this because regulators require it" beats a silent form.
- Split verification. Light check to explore, full check to transact.
- Celebrate the first win visibly. A confirmed trade, a connected card, a first saved goal.

The most expensive screen in your product is the one right before that first win. It deserves more design time than your homepage.

Which screen is standing between your users and theirs?
```

Pretty is a side effect of clear.

## B5 · Next gen products (AI, 2026)

```
Every AI product is adding an agent this quarter.

Almost none of them tell the user what the agent is about to do.

I've been testing AI features inside finance and productivity tools, and the pattern is the same: a sparkle icon, a text box, and a button that says "Generate" or "Run". Then something happens. Sometimes it's useful. Often the user doesn't know what changed, where, or how to undo it.

The products that feel next gen don't have more AI. They have more clarity around it:

- Preview before action. "Here's what I'll do. Confirm?"
- Visible scope. Which accounts, which files, which date range.
- A way back. Undo that works, not a warning that doesn't.
- Progress that means something. Not a spinner, a list of steps being completed.

Trust in an AI product isn't built by the model. It's built on the screen between the prompt and the result.

Which AI feature has earned your trust this year, and what did it do differently?
```

## B6 · Activate (value)

```
Activation is one screen.

Not a funnel, not a journey map. One screen where a new user decides to stay or leave.

In a trading app it's the first trade confirmation. In a Web3 game it's the moment the wallet connects and the game starts. In a sustainability app I worked on, it was the first completed eco-action, and weekly habit retention went from 22% to 38% once that moment was designed on purpose.

How I find it:
1. Look at the retention curve and find the behavior that separates people who come back from people who don't.
2. Find the screen where that behavior happens for the first time.
3. Count the screens between sign-up and that one. Cut what you can.
4. Make that screen the clearest, calmest screen in the product.

Most teams spend their best design hours on the homepage. The activation screen gets whatever time is left.

Flip it.

Which screen is your activation moment? If you're not sure, that's the first thing to find out.

Save this for your next retention review.
```

## B10 · Connect the wallet (Web3 proof)

```
Web3 doesn't have a crypto problem. It has a "Connect wallet" problem.

On a Web3 gaming platform I redesigned, about 4 in 10 wallet connections failed. Not because of the chain. Because of the screen.

Players who had never touched crypto were asked to connect a wallet before they saw a single game. No explanation, no path for people without a wallet, and an error message that said only that something went wrong.

After the redesign, wallet-connection success went from 58% to 81%. First-game completion rose by about a third. The main tasks in the lobby, marketplace and inventory went from 4 clicks to 2.

My checklist for any wallet flow:
- Can the user see the product before connecting anything?
- Does the screen say what happens next, in plain words?
- Is there a path for someone with no wallet at all?
- When connection fails, does the user know what to do next?
- Are the top tasks two clicks away, or four?

The players didn't need a lesson about blockchains. They needed to play.

Which Web3 product has the best onboarding you've seen?

Save the checklist for your next wallet flow.
```

## B11 · A design system is a speed decision (design systems)

```
A design system isn't a Figma file. It's a decision about how fast you want to ship.

On a FinTech portfolio app, after we built a shared web and mobile component library, design-to-dev handoff time dropped by about 30% and the shipping cycle went from 4 weeks to 2.5.

On a trading platform, a system of 60+ components cut the time to design a new feature by about 45%.

Most of that time comes from one thing: not making the same decision twice.

Every button your team redesigns from scratch is a small tax. On one screen it doesn't matter. Fifty screens and two platforms later, it's a sprint you'll never see on a roadmap.

You don't need a big system on day one. You need the 15 to 20 components your product uses every day, built properly, with the states your engineers keep asking about: loading, empty, error, disabled, dark mode.

Where does your team lose the most time between design and code?
```
