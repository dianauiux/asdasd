AI designs a screen in 10 seconds. So why hire a designer? Reel · talking · 20–25 s

Խոսելու տեքստ

0–3 վրկ · Էկրանին՝ AI designs a screen in 10 seconds. So why hire a designer?
Ձայն՝ AI can put a screen together in seconds. And honestly, it can look pretty good.
3–6 վրկ · Էկրանին՝ AI generating a UI
Ձայն՝ Clean layout, nice colors, everything looks finished.
6–12 վրկ · Էկրանին՝ You talking to camera
Ձայն՝ But who checks whether people can actually use it? Or why they leave halfway through?
12–16 վրկ · Էկրանին՝ User flow / research notes
Ձայն՝ Who talks to the team, asks the difficult questions, and decides what to change?
16–20 վրկ · Էկրանին՝ You talking to camera
Ձայն՝ That's still my work. Not just making screens, but making decisions.
20–24 վրկ · Էկրանին՝ AI is a tool. Judgment is the skill.
Ձայն՝ AI is a brilliant tool. I just don't hand over my judgment to it.

Նկարագրություն / Caption

AI can design a screen in 10 seconds.

It can't tell you why users leave at step three.
It can't hear what your support team hears every day.
It can't decide what to remove.

That's the job now. Not the pixels. The decisions behind them.

AI is my fastest tool. It's not my replacement.

Designers: which part of your job can AI still not do? 👇

"Small test task" LinkedIn + IG carousel

Նկարագրություն / Caption

One of my students sent me their "small test task" last week and asked if it was normal.

It was a full onboarding redesign. Four screens, a clickable prototype, and a presentation for the team. Deadline: Monday.

No, that's not normal. That's a project.

I'm not against test tasks. A short one helps both sides, and I'd rather see how someone thinks than just read a CV. But two or three hours is a test. Three days of unpaid work, for a role with 200 applicants, is something else.

And it filters out the wrong people. The designers with the least free time are often the ones already doing great work somewhere.

If you're hiring, keep it short, make the problem up instead of using your real backlog, and send everyone a few lines of feedback. People remember that.

What's the longest test task anyone has asked you to do?

Communication culture Carousel

Նկարագրություն / Caption

We spend a lot of time teaching designers how to talk to clients.

Be clear. Be polite. Reply on time. Explain your decisions.

All true. But we rarely talk about the other side of the conversation.

Respect in a project goes both ways. And most clients aren't being difficult on purpose. Nobody ever showed them how a designer works.

So I don't wait for problems. I set the rules before the project starts:

→ My working hours and when to expect a reply
→ One channel for feedback, not five
→ What "urgent" means, and what can wait until morning
→ How many revision rounds we have, and how to give useful feedback
→ When I'm in deep work and won't be checking messages

It takes 15 minutes on the first call. It saves weeks of frustration on both sides.

A client who knows how you work trusts you faster. A designer who explains how they work gets treated like a partner, not a vendor.

Clear boundaries don't create distance. They create respect.

Save this for your next kickoff call.

Designers: do you set these rules on day one, or do you learn them the hard way?
Clients: what do you wish your designer had told you at the start?
